# Daily Brief — Thursday, August 27, 2026

Nepal's flood death toll keeps climbing with hundreds still missing. A federal judge let Trump's mail-in voting rule stand while ruling USPS had defied a court order to get there. A San Bernardino County corruption probe and a water-fund dispute are the local threads this week.

## International

**1. Nepal-Tibet flash floods: death toll past 270, hundreds still missing.** A glacial collapse triggered flash floods Wednesday along the Trishuli/Bhotekoshi river system in Nepal's Rasuwa and Nuwakot districts. The death toll has climbed past 270 with roughly 1,500 people still missing, including hundreds of foreign tourists caught in the disaster zone. Landslides have dammed parts of the river, raising the risk of a second flood. (NPR)

**2. Pakistan hospital nursery fire kills 14 newborns.** An air-conditioning compressor exploded early Wednesday in the nursery of the gynecology ward at Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences in Islamabad, one of the country's deadliest hospital fires on record. One infant was rescued. PM Shehbaz Sharif removed the health secretary and ordered an investigation. (AP)

**3. Canada hits US with matching tariffs.** Ottawa announced roughly $20 billion in retaliatory tariffs on US goods, mirroring the 50% US tariff on $27.6 billion of Canadian exports imposed August 22. Finance Minister Champagne said Canada would match US tariffs "dollar for dollar, rate for rate." (NPR)

## National

**4. Judge lets Trump's mail-in voting rule stand, finds USPS defied a court order getting there.** US District Judge Indira Talwani declined to block the Postal Service's final rule tightening mail-in ballot handling, which requires states to supply USPS with lists of mail voters and apply unique barcodes to ballot envelopes. She ruled separately that USPS violated an existing court order by issuing the rule the way it did. Her injunction still bars USPS from rejecting noncompliant ballot mail before the 2026 midterms. (NPR, Axios)

**5. Court filings detail DHS undercover operation at churches and union halls.** Documents filed August 13 show DHS agents, under an operation that expanded after two people were killed in a January ICE raid, went undercover at meetings inside five Minnesota churches, including a Sunrise Movement event at First Universalist Church in Minneapolis, and subpoenaed financial records from the AFL-CIO and other unions. None of the groups have been charged with a crime. Civil liberties groups say the tactics raise First Amendment concerns about surveilling lawful political activity. (NPR)

**6. Meta settles for up to $18 billion over child social-media harms.** Meta reached a settlement with a bipartisan coalition of state attorneys general resolving claims it knowingly designed Instagram and Facebook to addict children and collected minors' data without parental consent. Meta will cap teen usage at two hours a day and block access from midnight to 6 a.m. absent parental consent, audited annually for five years. About 30% of the payout is contingent on YouTube, TikTok and Snapchat adopting similar limits. (NPR)

## Local and California

**7. Resident challenges Ayala Park design contract as a Prop 218 violation.** At the July 21 Chino City Council meeting, resident Stubbie Barr argued the city improperly used water-fund reserves to pay for the Ayala Park irrigation design contract, which he says violates Prop 218's rule that ratepayer fees can only cover water-service costs. The Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association has asked the city for a clear accounting of how the water fund reserves were used. (Champion, unrated local)

**8. Chino Police expand outreach at The Preserve apartment complex.** Twenty patrol officers, plus school resource officers from Cal Aero and Legacy academies, met with residents at the Homecoming Apartments after growing security concerns there. Councilman Marc Lucio said he is working to open a police substation in the Town Center at The Preserve. This follows the county-level fallout from the July 28 FBI search of Fourth District Supervisor Curt Hagman's home and office, which remains an open corruption investigation with possible foreign-influence angles. (Champion, unrated local)

The strongest case against the mail-in voting rule: critics argue the barcode and voter-list requirements function as a de facto restriction on mail voting dressed up as a design standard, that USPS proceeding despite an existing court order shows an administration willing to run past judicial checks ahead of a midterm election, and that a rule this consequential for ballot access deserves full judicial review rather than a narrow procedural finding. (NPR, Washington Post)
